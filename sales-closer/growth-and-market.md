# Growth, Market and Offer Strategy

Big-picture principles for business owners: who to sell to, how to grow, how to think about failure and how to make an offer hard to compare. Use this file when the user asks about strategy, pricing power, growth or why a business is stuck.

These are rules of thumb from public business teaching, not laws. Present them as strong guidance and adapt to the user's situation.

## 1. Choose your market before polishing your pitch

**Idea:** a good seller in a weak market loses to an average seller in a strong market. Check three things: Is the market growing? Do buyers have money? Is the problem painful and urgent?

**How to coach:**
- Ask: "Is your market growing, flat or shrinking?"
- If shrinking or flat, discuss moving the same skill to an adjacent, growing market.
- Skill transfers. A strong web designer who struggles with small cafés might thrive selling to clinics or law firms.

## 2. Start with buyers who can pay more

**Idea:** the crowded middle (not premium, not cheap) is the hardest place to compete. Selling first to buyers who value outcomes and can pay well gives you cash, better results to show and room to deliver great service. Serving price-sensitive buyers later works better once you have systems and volume.

**How to coach:**
- Help the user define a premium buyer: someone for whom the problem costs a lot.
- Position on results and service, not on being the cheapest.
- Warn against mid-priced "we're like everyone else" positioning.

**Honest caveat:** this is a strategy, not a guarantee. Premium selling needs proof and strong delivery.

## 3. Make the offer so strong that comparison fades

**Idea:** when the offer is clearly better in outcome, speed, support and risk terms, buyers stop comparing prices and start asking how to begin. Cheaper competitors then look like a different category.

**Build it by:**
- Targeting a specific result
- Adding support and speed that others lack
- Removing risk with a strong guarantee
- Packaging the solution so it can't be matched line by line

Test: if the buyer compared you to a cheaper option, could they say "they're basically the same"? If yes, your offer is too generic.

## 4. Passion is not business skill

**Idea:** loving your craft or your reason for starting doesn't mean you know how to run a business. Sales, marketing, pricing, operations and management are separate skills to learn.

**How to coach:**
- Respect the passion, then ask: "Which business skill is weakest right now: getting leads, closing, delivery, or finances?"
- Turn the weakest area into a 30-day learning goal (`training-plan.md`).

## 5. Learn from failure on purpose

**Idea:** understanding why something failed is more useful than not knowing why something worked. Winning by luck teaches little. A clear failure analysis teaches a lot.

**How to coach:** run a post-mortem after lost deals, failed launches or closed clients:
1. What was the goal?
2. What happened?
3. What was the real cause (offer, market, execution, pricing, timing)?
4. What will I change?
5. What will I test next?

Write the answer in one sentence and keep a log.

## 6. Growth is the default goal

**Idea:** a business that doesn't grow tends to decline: costs rise, competitors improve, customers leave. A useful reference is long-run market growth (around 7 to 10% a year). Staying flat means falling behind. Ambitious owners can aim much higher, such as 25% a year.

**Caution:** targets depend on the business and stage. Use them as benchmarks, not promises.

**How to coach:** ask for last year's revenue, this year's, and the gap. Then identify which lever from the next section to pull.

## 7. The three growth levers

A business grows by:
1. **Getting more customers** (leads, outreach, referrals, partnerships)
2. **Raising the average purchase value** (higher prices, premium tiers, bundles, upgrades)
3. **Getting customers to buy more often or more** (repeat purchases, renewals, add-ons, upsells)

**How to coach:** find the weakest lever. Many owners focus only on lever 1 when levers 2 and 3 are cheaper and faster, since the customers already exist.

Quick diagnostic:
| Question | If the answer is "low" |
|---|---|
| How many new customers per month? | Work on lead generation |
| What's the average sale? | Raise price, add tiers, bundle |
| How many buy again? | Add renewals, follow-ups, new offers |

## 8. Separate the offer from your self-worth

**Idea:** if an offer doesn't sell, that is information about the offer, market or message. It does not prove you are a failure. The only real failure is quitting while you could still adjust.

**How to coach:**
- When a user says "I'm bad at sales", reframe: "What did the buyers say? What does that tell us about the offer?"
- Turn each rejection into a test: change the offer, audience or message and run it again.
- Keep going. Persistence plus adjustment beats talent.

## 9. Pricing power

**Idea:** anyone can raise a price. The skill is raising it while buyers still say yes. That requires stronger value, proof, positioning and a better buying experience.

**Ways to earn pricing power:**
- Bigger or clearer outcomes
- Strong proof and case studies
- Faster results
- Stronger guarantees
- Premium service and brand
- Better targeting of buyers for whom the problem is expensive

When raising a price, improve something real at the same time. See `pricing-and-guarantees.md`.

## 10. Shorten the time to first result

**Idea:** people buy for the long-term outcome, but they stay and trust you because of early wins. The shorter the gap between buying and seeing value, the more valuable the offer feels and the lower the refunds and churn.

**How to apply:**
- Design a "first win" within days, not months.
- Deliver something visible quickly (a quick audit, a first draft, an early result).
- Show progress often (milestones, reports, check-ins).

Ask: "What's the first thing the customer can see or feel after paying?"

## 11. The four drivers of value

Covered in detail in `offer-and-value.md`. In short:
- **Raise:** the size of the dream outcome and the buyer's belief they'll achieve it.
- **Lower:** the time delay and the effort or sacrifice required.

Use this as the main checklist when an offer feels weak.

## 12. Offer stacking: one subject, many solutions, one price

**Idea:** a buyer with a big goal usually has many related problems. Identify each problem, create a solution for each, then present them together as one complete package at a price that feels clearly smaller than the total value. The buyer sees a full solution rather than one product.

**Steps:**
1. Pick one subject (e.g. "getting clients online").
2. List every problem in the way (no website, no traffic, no trust, no follow-up, no booking system).
3. Create a solution for each problem.
4. Name each component and state the problem it solves.
5. Estimate the real value of each component honestly.
6. Present the whole stack, then the price.
7. Add a guarantee.

**Example:**
- Problem: no professional website, solution: custom site
- Problem: no traffic, solution: local SEO setup
- Problem: no trust, solution: review collection system
- Problem: missed inquiries, solution: booking and auto-reply setup
- Problem: no tracking, solution: monthly performance report

**Honesty rules:**
- Every item must be something you'll actually deliver and that the buyer values.
- Value numbers must be defensible, not inflated.
- Don't pad the stack with filler. More is not better; relevant is better.
- The price should feel fair against the total benefit, not a trick.

## 13. Applying this in conversations

- **Strategy question:** start with the market, then the buyer, then the offer.
- **"My business is stuck":** ask the growth-lever diagnostic.
- **"Nobody buys":** question the offer and market before blaming the pitch.
- **Price pushback pattern:** check whether the offer is generic and can be compared.
- **User sounds discouraged:** use idea 8, then give one concrete test to run this week.
